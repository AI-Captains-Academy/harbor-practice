Most people think self-hosting means a server rack humming in their closet.

It doesn't. The fastest way to "I control everything" starts on someone else's machine... and that's not a compromise. It's the smart move.

This is Part One of the workshop — the *speedy way*. Rent a VPS, get something running today, and decide later whether you ever need more.

## What a VPS actually is

*VPS* stands for Virtual Private Server.

Plain version: a computer someone else owns, but you control.

Here's the mechanism. A data center is full of big physical servers. A piece of software called a *hypervisor* runs on each one and slices that machine into pieces. Your piece is your *virtual machine* — it acts like a real computer, has its own operating system, but it's sharing the physical box with a few other tenants.

(Think of it like an apartment building. One structure, separate lockable units. You never see your neighbors.)

The part that matters: you get *root access*. Your OS, your rules. Install whatever you want, configure it however you want.

## Why you start here (and probably stay)

I host every public-facing thing I own on a VPS. Nothing the world touches lives at my house.

Here's the honest list of reasons, because there's no hype in it:

**Reliable power and network.** If the power goes out at your house, your stuff goes down. At a data center, they have redundancy, backups, and a team that fixes outages before you even notice. (I've had storms kill my home internet for hours. The VPS never blinked.)

**No hardware to buy.** No router surgery on your home network. You pay $5–$20 a month and someone else owns the metal.

**Traffic.** Your home internet connection has an upload bottleneck. A VPS in a data center does not. If your site gets a spike, the VPS handles it. Your house... doesn't.

**Attack surface.** This one's important. If you host a public app at home and someone hacks it, they're inside your home network. They can log your traffic, poke at your other devices, do god-knows-what. If a VPS gets hacked, it's a throwaway box in a data center. Better the rented apartment than your actual house.

**GPU economics.** Want to run AI in the cloud? Rent a cloud GPU *by the hour*. Buying a comparable card runs $3,000... probably $5,000, maybe $10,000. And even then it's not as good as what you can rent. (The energy bill alone makes home AI impractical for most people.)

**Liability.** If someone uploads something illegal to your app, the abuse notice hits the *host*, not your house. The hosting company has procedures for this. At home, they might assume *you* hosted it.

**Geography.** Host in South America and your Asia visitors see lag. With a VPS you pick the region — EU users hit the EU server, US users hit the US server. At home you get one location: yours.

## The uncomfortable truth most people skip

The average person is probably fine with a VPS for life.

Start with a VPS. Stay with a VPS. The stuff that genuinely *needs* to be at home (which we get to in Module 2) is a shorter list than people think.

(That's the whole point of splitting this workshop into two parts. Part 1 is the 90% solution. Part 2 is the hobbyist 10%.)

## Key takeaways

- A *VPS* is a slice of a physical server in a data center — someone else owns the hardware, you get root access and full control.
- A *hypervisor* slices each physical machine into virtual machines; you control your slice.
- Start with a VPS and *stay* with one unless you have a specific reason to host at home — most people never need more.
- VPS wins on power, network, traffic capacity, attack surface, GPU cost, liability, and geography.
- The sovereignty filter still applies: you don't own the metal, but you own the *control*. That's the meaningful part.
