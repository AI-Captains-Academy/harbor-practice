## The decision tree (be honest with yourself)

If you host at home, *you* are responsible for everything. Power, network, backups, uptime. All of it.

So the first question isn't "what hardware" — it's "what happens when it goes down?"

My Bitcoin node? If the power goes out for a few hours, I'm fine without it. Not a big deal.

A business with thousands of users? Not fine. And I don't have power backups at my house. (A rack-mount UPS exists, but now we're adding cost and complexity.)

**Match the purpose to the risk.** If downtime is a minor inconvenience, home is fine. If downtime costs you money or trust, use a VPS.

## What hardware will you use?

Four real options, from cheapest to heaviest:

**Raspberry Pi 4 or 5.** A couple hundred dollars. Low power, quiet, upgradeable (you can add little screens, heat sinks, cases). This is where most people start and where a lot of people stay.

**An old laptop.** Ron demoed this a few months back and it's genuinely clever. Take an old MacBook, plug it in, stop it from sleeping when the lid's closed, run Docker on it. Two things make this great: the *battery is a built-in UPS* (power goes out, you've got a couple hours), and the *screen is a built-in console* (no SSH needed — open the lid, make changes right there). 

**A refurbished mini PC.** Dell Optiplex, Lenovo ThinkCenter, the endless micro PCs on Amazon. $100 to a few hundred dollars, varying RAM and CPU. More horsepower than a Pi, still quiet, still cheap.

**A real server.** When you need serious RAM, storage, or a GPU. Loud. Power hungry. (Easier to just rent a VPS at this point — but if you need it at home for data sovereignty, it's an option.)

## Match the hardware to the job

This is the part people get wrong. They buy a Pi to run an AI video editor. It can't.

My homepage, jordanurbs.com — I *could* host that on a Raspberry Pi at home. Low traffic, no database, behind a proxy. Any of these devices handles it.

An AI video editor app? Needs a GPU, serious RAM, a real server. A Pi will laugh at you.

(If you need a lot of RAM for local models — the Pi 5 goes up to 16 GB now. Mine are all 8 GB, one is 4 GB, and they're doing great. You get more done than you'd think.)

## The pro tip that saves you money

Start with something you already own.

Got an old laptop in a drawer? Plug it in. Try self-hosting on *that*. Once you start hitting its limitations — too slow, not enough RAM, can't run the thing you want — *then* buy something.

(Do not buy a $200 Pi and a $100 case and a $40 screen before you've hosted a single thing. I've watched people do this... the hardware gathers dust.)

## Key takeaways

- Home hosting means *you* own uptime — power, network, backups, all of it. Match the risk to the purpose.
- Four hardware tiers: **Raspberry Pi** (cheap, low power), **old laptop** (free, built-in UPS + console), **refurbished mini PC** (more power, still quiet), **real server** (GPU/heavy RAM, loud, costly).
- Match hardware to the job — a static site runs on a Pi; an AI video editor needs a GPU.
- **Start with hardware you already own.** Hit its limits first, *then* buy.
- The Pi 5 hits 16 GB RAM — enough for surprisingly capable local work.

## Try this

Hardware math, not hardware shopping. The numbers teach more than the shopping.

- [ ] Power math off your *real* utility bill: an old laptop pulling ~15W 24/7 runs about 11 kWh/mo. At typical US rates that's ~$1.50–$2.50/month. A Pi pulls ~3–5W — less than a dollar. Compare both to the $6 VPS. (Electricity rates vary by region — use your own kWh price.)
- [ ] Matching drill: assign a hardware tier to each of these 5 workloads — static site, media transcode, local LLM, Bitcoin node, agent runtime. Pi? Old laptop? Mini PC? Real server? Defend each choice.
- [ ] Scorecard: fill in the Level 3 cost cell. (This is the one that surprises people. The Pi might cost less to run than the VPS costs to rent.)

Then comment: **your device + its real monthly power cost.** (Pull the number from your utility bill, not a guess. Pennies matter when you're comparing to a VPS.)
