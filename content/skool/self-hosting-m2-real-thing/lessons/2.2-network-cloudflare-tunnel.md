# 🚇 2.2: The Network Foundation & the Cloudflare Tunnel Trick

**🎬 Clip:** `2.2-network-cloudflare-tunnel.mp4` (~9 min) · source 29:50–38:47

---

Here's the part that scares people off home hosting: exposing your home network to the internet.

Good news — you don't have to. There's a trick that lets the world reach your home server *without ever knowing your home IP*. It's the single most important concept in this module.

But first, the foundation underneath it.

## How your home network works

Your fiber connection comes in, hits your *router*. The router already serves your laptops, phones, Wi-Fi... and now it'll serve your server machines too.

One thing worth knowing: your smart home devices — the "spying light bulbs," the voice assistants, the cameras — they're all on that same router. (Ron and I had a laugh about this in the workshop, because it's true. They're spying.)

The fix is a *VLAN* — a virtual local area network that segments your IoT stuff onto its own isolated network so it can't see your other devices. Not required for this lesson, but file it away.

## Turn off UPnP. Use port forwarding.

*UPnP* (Universal Plug and Play) lets any device on your network automatically open holes in your router's firewall. Convenient for multiplayer games — the console opens the port it needs without you touching anything.

It's also a security hole. Any compromised device can punch a hole for attackers.

**Turn it off.** Use manual *port forwarding* instead — you decide exactly which ports open and where they route.

(If you turn off UPnP and something breaks, that's the trade-off. Security costs a little convenience. Worth it.)

## Dynamic DNS — when your IP keeps changing

Your ISP gives you a *public IP address*. Problem: it can change without warning. So `home.yourdomain.com` might point at the right house today and a stranger's tomorrow.

*Dynamic DNS* (DDNS) fixes this. A small client on your network reports its current public IP to a DNS service. When the IP changes, the service updates your domain automatically. Visitors always reach the right place.

(Ron's clean version: your internal laptop IPs are different from your internet IP — the router sits between them. DDNS just keeps the domain pointing at the *router's* current public address. That's it.)

Honestly... your AI will handle all of this. Set up the Pi with an SSH key, tell it what you want, and it configures DDNS for you. But you should know what it's *doing*.

## The Cloudflare Tunnel trick (this is the big one)

Here's how you host a real website at home *without exposing your home* (the production version needs a free Cloudflare account and your own domain — the 5-minute drill below uses the no-account test version so you can feel it first):

1. A small program (the Cloudflare *daemon*, `cloudflared`) runs on your server.
2. It *dials out* to Cloudflare — an outbound connection, like a phone call you initiated.
3. When a visitor comes to your website, they hit *Cloudflare*, not your house.
4. Cloudflare routes their traffic back through that outbound connection to your server.

**Your router forwards nothing.** Port scanners see a brick wall... your home IP is invisible to the world.

(Cloudflare also does caching — so a lot of requests never even reach your machine. Less load on your Pi, more headroom for traffic spikes.)

## The demo: a Bitcoin payment server on my Pi

This is one of the only things I host at home that faces the public.

When someone pays in Bitcoin on the AI Captains site, the invoice generation, the whole flow — that's *BTCPay Server running on a Raspberry Pi in my house*, routed through a Cloudflare tunnel.

Why at home and not a VPS? Two reasons:

**Privacy.** I'd rather not entrust a third party with payment data. This one I want to own.

**The blockchain.** Running a Bitcoin payment server means running a *Bitcoin node* — which means storing a *terabyte* of blockchain data somewhere. That's not practical on a $6 VPS. At home, it's just a hard drive.

(Low traffic too. Almost nobody pays in Bitcoin yet. If it went viral and crashed, I'd know exactly why — and it wouldn't cost me my home network, because the tunnel hides everything.)

## Cloudflare for public, Tailscale for private

One clean rule:

**Cloudflare** = public-facing things other people touch (your website, your payment page).

**Tailscale** (or WireGuard) = private things only *you* touch (your home dashboard, your camera feeds, your AI agent).

Don't expose your home assistant through Cloudflare. Don't route your public website through Tailscale. Each tool has a job.

## Where you'll use this

The agents you build in the main curriculum need to be reachable. If they're *private* (just you talking to your AI from your phone), Tailscale is the answer — we cover it in **2.3**. If they're *public* (a payment page, a webhook endpoint), the Cloudflare tunnel is the answer. This lesson is the network layer that makes both safe.

## Key takeaways

- Your router serves everything on your network — segment IoT devices with a *VLAN* so the "spying light bulbs" can't see your real devices.
- Turn off *UPnP* (auto port-holes = security risk). Use manual *port forwarding*.
- *Dynamic DNS* keeps your domain pointed at the right IP when your ISP changes it. Your AI can configure it, but know what it does.
- The **Cloudflare Tunnel**: your server dials out to Cloudflare; visitors hit Cloudflare, not your house. Port scanners see a brick wall.
- **Cloudflare for public, Tailscale for private** — never cross them.
